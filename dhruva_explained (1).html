How DHRUVA works, explained from zero

Every idea here is written first in everyday language, with the technical name in brackets right after it. Read it straight through once and the architecture should make sense without any prior background.

The technical names matter for one reason only: a jury will use them, and you need to recognise them when they do.

Section 1The whole idea in one page

Phone navigation works because your phone listens to satellites. Go into a tunnel and the concrete blocks that signal, so the app freezes or the dot jumps around.

Expensive cars solve this with sensors wired to the wheels. Most vehicles in India — trucks, older cars, two-wheelers — have nothing but a driver's phone on the dashboard. So the challenge is to keep tracking position using only what a bare phone can feel: shaking, tilting, turning.

The technical name for tracking position this way is dead reckoning (working out where you are from your last known position plus your speed, direction and elapsed time — no external reference).

Everyday version

You walk out of a room, close your eyes, and take twenty steps forward, turn right, take ten more. You can still point roughly at where the door is. You didn't see anything — you kept track of your own movement. That's dead reckoning.

Now notice what happens if your step length is slightly off, or your "right turn" was really 80 degrees. After twenty steps the error is small. After five hundred, you're nowhere near where you think.

That last part is the entire engineering problem. Cheap phone sensors are slightly wrong, and small errors compound into large ones fast. Everything in this system exists to fight that.

Section 2What your dataset actually is

Your download contains 564 spreadsheet files in two kinds.

KindCountWhat's in it
S files241What the phone felt — accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, GPS readings, taken many times a second during real drives
V files323The same drives recorded from the car's own computer — real wheel speeds, real speedometer, steering angle, gear, engine RPM

The S files are the question. The V files are the answer key. That single sentence explains the whole training process.

Everyday version

You're teaching someone to guess a car's speed with their eyes shut. You let them feel the ride and ask "how fast?" They say sixty. You glance at the speedometer: it was fifty-two. You tell them. Repeat a few million times and they get genuinely good at it.

The S files are the ride. The V files are the speedometer you check against.

The formal name for this setup is supervised learning (training a model on examples where the correct answer is already known, so the error can be measured and corrected). The known-correct values are the ground truth (the reference measurements you treat as correct — here, the car's own sensors).

Rule you cannot break

Once trained, the V files are thrown away. In a real vehicle there is no car computer to ask — that's the entire premise of the problem statement. If your model ever sees real speed while being tested, your results are meaningless. This mistake is called data leakage (accidentally letting information from the answer key reach the model at inference time), and it is exactly the kind of thing a sharp jury asks about.

One practical note: the files use six slightly different column layouts, and some name the gyroscope axes X/Y/Z while others use Yaw/Pitch/Roll. Don't assume those are interchangeable without checking — mismatched axes will quietly poison your training data.

Section 3The three phone sensors

Everything the system knows comes from three small chips. Together they're called an IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit — the sensor package that measures motion and rotation). Phone-grade ones are MEMS (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems — tiny, cheap, and noticeably noisier than the industrial kind).

Accelerometer — feels pushes

When a bus brakes and you lurch forward, that lurch is what this measures. It also feels gravity constantly pulling down, which turns out to be very useful.

Gyroscope — feels turning

The twist you feel in your body when a car corners. It reports rate of rotation, not direction — it knows you're turning right now, not which way you're pointing.

Magnetometer — a compass

Points north, same as a needle compass. Reliable outdoors, easily confused by metal and electronics — including the car body itself.

Your S files record all three, along three axes each. The axis names are pitch (nose tilting up or down), roll (tilting side to side) and yaw (rotating like a compass needle, flat).

Section 4The runtime flow

This is what happens on the phone, continuously, while driving. Four processing stages between the sensors and the screen.

The single most important thing on that diagram: GPS joins at stage 3, not stage 1. Stages 1 and 2 behave identically whether or not there is any satellite signal at all. Nothing upstream has to be reconfigured when you enter a tunnel — one input to stage 3 simply stops arriving.

That is the honest technical explanation for the word "seamless" in your pitch. There is no mode switch to fumble, because the inertial system was running the whole time.

Section 5Module 1 — which way is the phone facing

Official name: in-vehicle alignment and calibration engine. Trained model? No.

Everyday version

You're blindfolded in a car and I tell you we just accelerated forward. But you're sitting sideways in your seat, so the push you feel isn't on your front — it lands on your shoulder. To make sense of it you first need to know how you're turned.

The phone has exactly this problem. It's clamped in a cradle at some arbitrary angle. When the car accelerates forward, the phone feels a push along a diagonal of its own axes, not along "forward". Work out its angle and you can correct every reading that follows.

The angle is called the phone's orientation (its pitch, roll and yaw relative to the world), and correcting readings with it is a frame transformation (rewriting measurements from the phone's own axes into the vehicle's axes).

Trick one — gravity tells you which way is down

Gravity always pulls straight down. Whichever direction the phone feels a constant tug is down, and from that you get how much it's tilted. Same as knowing which way is up in a pitch-dark room because you can feel your own weight.

Trick two — braking tells you which way is forward

A car can only really accelerate and brake along one line: forwards and backwards. It cannot shove itself sideways. So the module watches for hard braking and hard accelerating, notes which direction those pushes come from, and concludes that must be the vehicle's forward axis.

The statistical method for finding that dominant direction in the data is PCA (Principal Component Analysis — finds the axis along which a set of measurements varies most).

Where "Madgwick" comes from

Sebastian Madgwick published a recipe in 2010 for combining the three sensors into an orientation estimate. Saying "we use the Madgwick filter" is like saying "we use Pythagoras' theorem" — it's a person's name attached to a method, not a mysterious technology.

His recipe is needed because each sensor alone is inadequate:

  • The gyroscope is smooth and fast but slowly wanders off, like a clock that loses a few seconds every hour. The technical term is drift (error that accumulates steadily over time).
  • The accelerometer gives an absolute reference through gravity, but gets confused whenever the car itself is accelerating — because then it's feeling gravity plus the car's motion.
  • The magnetometer gives absolute heading but is disturbed by the steel around it.

The recipe blends all three so each covers the others' weaknesses. It has one tuning dial, written as beta (controls how much the filter trusts gravity and the compass versus the gyroscope). Turn it up and it corrects faster but picks up more noise; turn it down and it's smoother but drifts more.

Likely question — why not use Android's built-in orientation sensor?

Because it's a vendor black box that behaves differently on every phone, we can't tune it for sustained vehicle acceleration, and the edge-deployable engine has to run on external hardware that isn't Android at all. We do use it as a sanity check to validate our own implementation against.

Honest weakness worth naming yourself

In a moving vehicle the accelerometer is feeling gravity, the car's acceleration and chassis vibration all at once — while the method assumes it's mostly gravity. Standard fixes: smooth the input, and make beta adaptive so the filter leans on the gyroscope during hard driving. Being ready with this is better than being caught without it.

Section 6Module 2 — how fast are we going

Official name: AI speed and vibration filter. Trained model? Yes — this is your main one.

You have no speedometer. The phone cannot plug into the car. So where does speed come from?

The obvious approach, and exactly why it fails

Physics says you can add up acceleration over time to get speed. That process is called integration (accumulating a rate over time to get a total — acceleration adds up to speed, speed adds up to distance).

Suppose the sensor always reads 0.01 too high — a trivial error. It has a name: sensor bias (a constant offset in a sensor's readings). Watch what happens when you accumulate it while the car is parked:

  • 1 secondoff by a hair
  • 10 secondsyou "read" 0.4 km/h while stationary
  • 60 secondsyou "read" 2 km/h while stationary
  • 5 minutesyou believe you're driving. You're parked.

And you then integrate that speed again to get distance, so the error compounds twice over. The formal description is that positional error grows quadratically (proportional to time squared — doubling the time quadruples the error).

If you remember one thing about this project, remember this: small sensor errors do not stay small. Accumulating a slightly-wrong number makes it very wrong very quickly. Every other module exists because of this fact.

The approach that actually works

Everyday version

You can roughly tell how fast a car is going with your eyes closed. The engine note, the tyre roar, how much the seat buzzes. Thirty and a hundred feel nothing alike — and you're not doing any arithmetic. You're recognising a feeling.

The phone can sense all of that. At 60 km/h the engine rhythm, tyre hum and suspension bounce genuinely form a different pattern than at 20 km/h. So rather than calculating speed, the model recognises it: this pattern of shaking means roughly 60.

Framed technically, this treats speed estimation as a regression (predicting a continuous number from an input, rather than picking a category) problem over a window (a short fixed-length slice of consecutive sensor readings) of IMU data.

The crucial advantage: nothing is being accumulated. Each guess is made fresh. A bad guess doesn't make the next guess worse, so errors never snowball.

What CNN-LSTM means — two parts, two jobs

CNN (Convolutional Neural Network — scans across data looking for local patterns) is the pattern spotter. It looks at a slice of shaking and picks out shapes: that sharp jolt is a pothole, that steady buzz is the engine, that rumble is road texture. Similar to how you can pick a friend's voice out of a noisy room without analysing sound waves.

LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory — a network that carries information forward across a sequence) is the memory. Speed cannot jump: if you were doing 60 a second ago, you are not doing 5 now. So when the pattern spotter has a bad moment, the memory overrules it. Like a friend saying "you can't have driven 200 km in ten minutes."

One sees patterns; the other keeps the answer sensible over time.

Note that recognising a pothole is the same skill as learning to ignore one — a pothole is a big jolt that says nothing about your speed. That's why this module is called a vibration filter as well as a speed model.

How it gets trained and shipped

Windows of S-file sensor data go in, V-file speeds are the target, and the error between prediction and truth is minimised. That error measure is the loss function (the number the training process tries to make as small as possible). The finished model is shrunk for phone use by quantization (storing the model's numbers at lower precision so it runs faster and smaller with little accuracy cost) and exported to TFLite (TensorFlow Lite — the format for running trained models on phones).

Why this module is the one that matters

The problem statement's central constraint is no physical connection to the vehicle. This model is what satisfies it — it replaces the speedometer. Without a speed estimate, dead reckoning has no distance term and the whole system collapses. Everything else is refinement; this is load-bearing.

Honest weakness worth naming yourself

The model learns the vibration signatures present in its training data. A vehicle or road surface unlike anything in the dataset will degrade its accuracy. This is a generalisation (how well a model performs on data unlike what it trained on) limit — and note that the problem statement deliberately supplies extra datasets at evaluation precisely to test it.

Section 7Module 3 — where are we, all told

Official name: GNSS+INS fusion engine. Trained model? Mostly classical, with a small trained part.

This is the bookkeeper, and the only module that holds an actual answer to "where are we right now". Everything else either feeds it or reads from it.

Everyday version

You're walking to a friend's house you've been to before. Two sources of information:

Your own sense of progress. "I've walked about ten minutes, so I'm near the park." Always available, but the longer you go without checking, the less sure you get.

A street sign, occasionally. Definitive when you see one — but only appears sometimes, and can be a bit misleading.

So you keep a running estimate and adjust whenever a sign appears. And crucially: how much you adjust depends on how confident you already were. Just saw a sign a minute ago and the new one disagrees slightly? Ignore most of it. Been walking blind for half an hour? Trust the sign completely.

That is the entire mechanism. Your own sense of progress is the phone sensors; the street signs are GPS. The module keeps a best guess plus a how-sure-am-I number, and blends the two according to their relative confidence.

The formal names: the best guess is the state (the set of numbers being tracked — position, speed, orientation, sensor biases), and the how-sure-am-I number is the covariance (the filter's own estimate of how uncertain it currently is). The loop has two halves — predict (move the estimate forward using the sensors; uncertainty grows) and update (pull the estimate toward a new measurement; uncertainty shrinks).

Why a tunnel doesn't break it

In a tunnel you simply stop seeing street signs. You keep walking, keep estimating, and grow steadily less certain. On exit you see a sign and correct. You never stopped walking. In the same way, the predict half keeps running off the sensors and only the update half goes quiet.

Where the name comes from

A Kalman filter (the standard method for combining a running estimate with occasional noisy measurements) is named after Rudolf Kálmán, who invented it in 1960; it guided Apollo to the moon. Another person's name attached to a recipe.

The Unscented variant (a version built to handle curved, non-straight-line relationships) is used because turning a corner involves angles, and angles bend the maths in ways the basic version handles clumsily. It works by pushing a carefully chosen spread of sample points — sigma points (representative samples that capture the current estimate and its uncertainty) — through the real equations instead of approximating them.

The older alternative is the EKF (Extended Kalman Filter — approximates the curves with straight lines using hand-derived calculus). UKF avoids that derivation work, handles the large uncertainty of a long outage better, and is the variant your problem statement names explicitly.

The quietly clever part

The state includes the sensors' own error terms — their bias values. So while GPS is available, every update is implicitly noting "my sensor estimate ran three percent high compared to GPS" and remembering it. By the time you enter the tunnel, the filter already knows your specific phone's quirks and corrects for them.

Worth saying out loud — it sounds smart because it is

Driving with good GPS actively makes the tunnel work better. Signal-available stretches aren't just when the system works — they're when it calibrates itself for the outage that hasn't happened yet.

Where the small AI part sits

The filter needs to decide how much to trust GPS. Conventionally that's hand-tuned once and left alone — which is the weak point, because GPS between skyscrapers deserves far less trust than GPS on an open highway, and a fixed setting treats them the same. Bad reflected signals like that are called multipath (satellite signal arriving via bounces off buildings, so the reported position is wrong).

A small trained network predicts the trust level per moment instead, from satellite count and reported accuracy — both of which are columns in your S files.

Section 8Module 4 — cars are on roads

Official name: map matching and kinematic constraints. Trained model? No.

Even with everything above, you slowly drift. It's unavoidable with cheap sensors. This module provides a correction that comes from no sensor at all.

Everyday version

Someone tells you a friend is standing forty metres inside a building. You wouldn't accept it — you'd say they must be on the road next to it. Cars are on roads. Not in buildings, not in rivers, not hovering.

That's free information, and the module has a road map stored on the phone to use it. Your estimate drifts into a building, it gets nudged back to a sensible road.

Why simple snapping isn't enough

Naive approach: each moment, snap to the nearest road. On a highway with a parallel service road, your wobbling estimate snaps highway, service road, highway, service road — your path teleports back and forth and looks broken.

Better question: what single continuous drivable route best explains the last thirty seconds? You cannot teleport between roads, so it commits to one and stays consistent.

Everyday version

Reading messy handwriting. You don't decode each letter in isolation — you read the whole word and pick the reading that makes sense. Individual letters that looked ambiguous resolve themselves once the word does.

The technique for finding the most likely sequence rather than the most likely individual guess is an HMM (Hidden Markov Model — works out the most probable chain of hidden states from a series of noisy observations), and the algorithm that searches it is Viterbi (finds the single highest-probability path through all the possibilities). The road map itself comes from OpenStreetMap (a free, openly licensed world map you can download and use offline).

The phrase to not be scared of

Non-holonomic constraints (the rule that a vehicle can only move along its own heading — it cannot slide sideways or lift off the road).

That's all it means: cars can't do certain things. Any estimate implying a sideways slide gets discarded immediately as physically impossible. It's a cheap rule that deletes a whole category of wrong answers — and sideways drift is exactly what accumulating sensor error tends to produce.

If asked to define the term

It means a vehicle can only move along its own heading — it can't translate sideways. We use that to reject position estimates that would be physically impossible for a car.

This module is your best answer to how you'll meet the benchmark. The sensors alone probably won't hold under ten percent drift over a kilometre. The sensors constrained to a road network will.

Section 9Module 5 — is GPS trustworthy right now

Official name: seamless GNSS deficit handler. Trained model? No.

Small module, not really an algorithm — a judgment call made continuously. It watches two things your dataset already records: how many satellites are visible, and how accurate GPS itself claims to be. When those degrade, it tells the bookkeeper to stop listening. When they recover, it resumes.

Because the predict half of the filter never stopped, there is no mode to switch into. The technical framing worth adopting: this is one continuously running inertial system that accepts GPS corrections when available, not two modes with a switch between them.

The genuinely hard engineering detail

Coming out of a tunnel is trickier than going in. After a kilometre blind your estimate might be eighty metres off. If the first GPS reading is applied at full strength, the dot visibly jumps — the exact ugly behaviour you're claiming to fix.

The filter handles this correctly if the uncertainty bookkeeping is right: after a long outage it knows it's unsure, so it eases across rather than snapping. If a jury asks what your hardest problem is, this is an excellent honest answer.

Section 10Module 6 — what the user sees

Official name: real-time navigation interface. Trained model? No.

Position rendered on an offline map, updating ten times a second, with movement smoothed between updates by interpolation (filling in intermediate positions so motion looks continuous rather than stepping). Two features from your deck live here.

The confidence circle

Instead of a dot pretending to be exact, a circle that grows as certainty falls. In a long tunnel it visibly widens.

This is a strong feature for a reason worth stating: you already have the number. The bookkeeper tracks its own uncertainty as part of ordinary operation, so you're surfacing something you have rather than inventing a display. It's also more honest than the alternative — Google Maps shows a confident dot even while guessing, which is why it feels like it's lying to you underground.

Voice guidance that keeps talking

The route and road map are on the phone, so it keeps saying "turn left in 200 metres" in a tunnel. Nothing needs downloading to know the next turn.

This module is what makes the work visible. The problem statement's opening complaint is user-facing — apps that freeze and jump — and a jury judges whether that's fixed by watching the marker, not by reading your architecture.

Section 11Training and deployment

All the heavy work happens before the demo, on a computer. Only the finished models run on the phone.

On dataset size: the full set runs into tens of gigabytes across hundreds of files, which is far too much to load into memory at once. The standard fix is a streaming loader (reads and processes small batches straight off disk as training runs, rather than loading everything first).

For the proposal stage this barely matters — the problem statement only asks for results from a subset. One file is enough for a preliminary model and a position plot.

Demo-day discipline

Bundle the models and the map before judging. Fetching either one live would quietly reintroduce the exact internet dependency your entire pitch argues against — in front of the people evaluating that claim.

Section 12GPS is not the internet

This distinction is easy to blur and important to get right, because a jury may test it.

GPSInternet
What it isYour phone listening to satellitesYour phone talking to servers
DirectionOne-way, receive onlyTwo-way
Needs a SIM?No. Works in airplane mode.Yes
Why it fails in a tunnelConcrete blocks the satellite signalNo cell tower coverage

In a tunnel you usually lose both, but for unrelated reasons. Your problem statement is about losing GPS. The umbrella term for satellite positioning generally is GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite System — covers GPS, Galileo, India's NavIC and others).

So how does the map keep working?

You download it in advance. The roads for your area are saved onto the phone's storage before the drive, in a format called MBTiles (a single file holding pre-rendered map tiles, readable straight from local storage). The app reads it locally, like opening a photo you already saved.

Nothing is being fetched, so nothing can fail.

Phrase it exactly like this

Our app makes zero internet requests while navigating.

Not "it degrades gracefully offline". It never needed the internet to begin with — a much stronger claim, and one that also means no paid map API keys.

Section 13Which parts are actually AI

Only two things are trained models. Being precise about this makes you look better, not worse.

ModuleTrained?What it is instead
1 · Which way is it facingNoMadgwick's recipe plus geometry
2 · How fast are we goingYesCNN-LSTM — your main model
3 · Where are we, all toldPartlyClassical filter with a small trained network for trust levels
4 · Cars are on roadsNoMap lookup plus probability
5 · Is GPS trustworthyNoThreshold checks on satellite count and accuracy
6 · What the user seesNoApp interface
Expect this question: "where's the AI? this looks like classical engineering"

We used trained models exactly where traditional methods fail — estimating speed with no speedometer, which has no closed-form equation, and judging sensor trust, which is conventionally just guessed once. We deliberately kept the rest classical, because those are solved problems and a proven method is more reliable than a network we'd have to justify.

Better than claiming everything is AI. It shows you know why each piece is what it is.

And the follow-up: "why not one big neural network for everything?"

It would need far more training data than this dataset provides, wouldn't transfer across the different sensor grades the problem statement requires, and would give us no uncertainty estimate — which we need, because our interface shows a confidence circle.

Section 14Answers to expect to give

Short forms of the points scattered above, gathered for revision.

Why does GPS only enter at stage 3?

Because it's the only stage that can use it. Stage 1 asks which way the phone is tilted — GPS has no opinion on that. Stage 2 must learn speed from shaking alone, or it would only work when GPS exists, which defeats the purpose. Stage 3 is a filter whose whole job is blending an estimate with occasional measurements, and GPS is an occasional measurement. Stage 4 works on the finished answer.

How is the transition seamless?

There's nothing to transition. The inertial system runs continuously; GPS is an optional input that stops arriving. Stages 1 and 2 behave identically either way, so nothing is reconfigured mid-drive.

How do you hit under ten percent drift over a kilometre?

Not with the sensors alone. The road-network constraint is what closes the gap — a drifting estimate gets bound to a physically drivable route.

What's your hardest engineering problem?

Re-acquisition on tunnel exit. After a long outage the first GPS reading may disagree substantially, and applying it at full strength makes the marker jump — the exact defect we're claiming to fix.

What's the weakest part of your pipeline?

The orientation filter assumes the accelerometer is mostly reading gravity, which is least true during hard driving. Mitigated by smoothing the input and adapting the filter's trust dial.

Why no paid map service?

Offline OpenStreetMap data removes the need for any live map API, which means no keys, no billing, and no connectivity dependency — consistent with the rest of the design.
